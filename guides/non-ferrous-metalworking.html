Non-Ferrous Metalworking

Master techniques for smelting, casting, and shaping copper, bronze, brass, lead, tin, and aluminum. Essential for creating coins, weapons, decorative items, and precision tools in civilization rebuilding.

Copper Smelting and Working

Copper is the most versatile non-ferrous metal for early civilization. It's relatively easy to smelt, work, and alloy. Native copper deposits can be worked cold; ore requires smelting.

Melting Point: 1085°C (1985°F). Copper melts at achievable temperatures in a good charcoal furnace or with primitive blowers.

Simple Copper Smelting Process

Ore Preparation: Crush ore to powder. The finer the powder, the more complete the smelting. Separate as much waste rock as possible manually—hand-picking large pieces of rock saves fuel.

Roasting: Heat ore in a kiln or fire to 500-700°C for 2-3 hours. This removes water and sulfur compounds, making smelting easier. Ore changes color from green to darker red-brown.

Furnace Requirements: A simple shaft furnace (6-8 feet tall, 18 inches diameter) works well. Layer charcoal and roasted ore (3:1 ratio charcoal to ore). Blow air through a bellows at the base. Copper collects at the furnace bottom.

Smelting Process: After 2-3 hours at temperature, copper liquefies at the bottom. Remove a clay plug at the tap hole and pour molten copper into an iron vessel or basin. A successful smelt yields dark red copper, though refined product requires remelting and casting.

Copper Impurities and Refining

Smelted copper contains oxides and impurities. Refining removes these through "poling"—inserting a wooden pole into molten copper. Gases from the burning wood reduce oxides. When the pole no longer produces gas bubbles, copper is refined. Refined copper pours smoothly and hardens to clean, workable metal.

Cold Working Copper

Pure copper is soft enough to work cold (no heating needed), though heating makes it more ductile. Hammer annealed (heated) copper into sheets, wire, or shapes. Reheat when metal becomes hard and brittle.

Annealing Temperature: Heat copper to dull red (400-600°C) and cool slowly. Rapid cooling causes brittleness. This softens the metal for continued working.

Uses: Copper vessels, decorative items, electrical wire (in advanced societies), jewelry, and—when alloyed—weapons and tools.

Bronze and Brass Alloying

Alloying copper with tin (bronze) or zinc (brass) creates stronger, harder metals suitable for weapons, tools, and structural applications.

Bronze Production

Composition: Bronze is 88-92% copper and 8-12% tin by weight. Higher tin (up to 20%) creates brittle but harder bronze for special applications. Lower tin (5%) creates softer, more workable bronze.

Tin Addition Method: Melt copper in a crucible to 1150°C (well above copper melting point). Add tin gradually—tin vaporizes if added too quickly. Stir well with a wooden stick. The mixture becomes bright and fluid when properly alloyed.

Brass Production

Composition: Brass is 60-90% copper and 10-40% zinc. Yellow brasses (65-90% copper) are common. Red brasses (80-90% copper, 10-20% zinc) are softer and more ductile.

Zinc Acquisition: Zinc ore (calamine) is less common than tin. Brass can be made by heating copper with calamine ore, though this is less efficient than alloying molten copper with molten zinc. In post-industrial settings, salvaging brass items and remelting them is the easiest approach.

Zinc Addition: Molten zinc vaporizes easily (boils at 908°C). Add zinc gradually to prevent loss and allow safe vaporization. The resulting liquid should be bright and fluid.

Working Brass: Brass is harder than copper but remains workable. It resists corrosion better than copper, making it ideal for items exposed to moisture. Brass loses zinc at high temperatures, so avoid prolonged heating above 600°C.

Lead Casting

Lead is soft, dense, and easy to cast. While toxic if ingested or inhaled as powder, lead is valuable for weights, fishing sinkers, bullets, and decorative casting.

Health Warning: Lead is toxic. Avoid touching mouth or eating near lead work. Wear gloves. Do not inhale lead dust or fumes. Lead oxide fumes are extremely dangerous—never heat lead above 500°C.

Simple Lead Smelting: Galena smelting is remarkably simple. Heat galena ore with charcoal in a furnace. Lead drips to the bottom within 1-2 hours. Pour molten lead into cast iron molds.

Melting Point: Lead melts at 327°C—easily achieved in any furnace capable of smelting copper. This makes lead production accessible even with primitive equipment.

Lead Casting Techniques

Mold Materials: Use clay, stone, or cast iron molds. Simple clay molds: press objects into moist clay to create impressions, then remove objects. Pour molten lead into the cavity. Allow to cool completely before removing (allow 1-2 hours for large castings).

Lost-Wax Casting: Carve a shape from beeswax. Create a clay mold around the wax shape. Heat the mold to 200°C—wax melts and drains away, leaving an exact cavity. Pour molten lead into the cavity.

Cooling: Lead cools quickly (within minutes) but must cool completely before handling. Hot lead can cause severe burns.

Lead Working After Casting

Lead is soft enough to hammer cold. Filing, shaping, and engraving work easily on lead. Lead oxidizes to gray-white (oxide coating) in air—this is normal and protective. Occasionally clean with leather to maintain appearance if desired.

Tin Extraction

Tin is less common than copper but essential for bronze. Tin ore is usually found with copper ore in ore-rich regions.

Tin Ore Identification

CassiteriteBlack or brown, heavy, metallic75-80%Specific gravity 7.0, very heavy
StanniteGrayish metallic27-28%Often with copper ore

Identification Test: Cassiterite is extremely heavy for its size. A fist-sized piece weighs as much as much larger rock. This density is distinctive.

Tin Smelting

Furnace Setup: Similar to copper smelting—a shaft furnace works well. Roast cassiterite ore at 700-800°C for several hours to remove water and improve reduction efficiency.

Smelting Process: Layer roasted ore with charcoal (4:1 charcoal to ore). Blow continuous air through bellows. Tin melts at 232°C—it liquefies before copper at much lower temperatures. Tin drips to the furnace bottom and can be tapped into molds.

Purity: Smelted tin often contains iron and other impurities. Remelting in a crucible and allowing impurities to settle improves purity. Skim off surface impurities before casting into molds.

Tin Uses

Pure tin is soft and brittle but valuable as an alloy component. The main use is bronze production. Additionally, tin coats copper to prevent corrosion (tinned copper items); tin foil provides light-weight wrapping, and tin solders are essential for joining metals.

Aluminum Recovery from Salvage

In post-industrial settings with abundant scrap metal, aluminum salvage and recasting is far easier than primary smelting (which requires electrolysis). Aluminum is valuable for its light weight and corrosion resistance.

Spark IgnitionBurns brightly whiteMagnesium; no sparks means aluminum

Common Aluminum Sources: Window frames, automotive parts, aircraft components, beverage cans, and cooking vessels. Separate aluminum from attached fasteners or non-aluminum materials before melting.

Melting Aluminum Scrap

Melting Point: 660°C (easily achieved). Aluminum oxidizes readily, forming a floating gray-white oxide layer. This layer (dross) must be skimmed off or the casting will be porous.

Crucible Material: Use iron or ceramic crucibles. Never use aluminum crucibles—they melt before the metal inside. Graphite crucibles work but are expensive.

Flux: Add a small amount of borax or salt to molten aluminum to help oxide removal. Stir gently—avoid folding in oxide. Allow the metal to sit for 1-2 minutes, then skim dross carefully.

Casting Aluminum

Mold Requirements: Aluminum flows easily, so molds can be detailed. Clay, plaster, or sand molds work well. Because aluminum melts at lower temperature than copper or bronze, smaller furnaces suffice.

Cooling: Aluminum cools faster than copper. Castings are usually hard within 10-15 minutes but should sit longer (1+ hours) before handling to ensure complete solidification.

Aluminum Alloys

Pure aluminum is soft. Alloy with copper (up to 10%) for strength, or zinc (up to 5%) for hardness. Higher copper content increases hardness but reduces ductility. Aluminum-copper alloys (duraluminum) can be heat-treated for additional strength.

Soldering and Brazing Techniques

Joining two pieces of metal without melting the base metal is essential for creating complex items, repairing, and building structures.

Soldering (Soft Solder)

Soft solder composition: Typically 50% lead and 50% tin, melting at 190°C. This is much lower than the metals being joined, allowing joints without weakening the base metal.

Plumber's Solder5050190°CModerate (water-tight)
Electronics Solder3763183°CStrong (electrical)
Pewter Solder0100232°CModerate (decorative)

Flux: A chemical (rosin, borax paste, or acid) cleans the joint, improving solder flow. Rosin flux is safest for most work; borax paste works at higher temperatures. Apply flux before soldering.

Soldering Process: 1. Heat the joint (both metal pieces) to 200-220°C with a hot tool (soldering iron, heated copper) 2. Apply solder to the hot joint—it should flow smoothly when the joint is hot enough 3. Remove heat and allow joint to cool without movement 4. Clean off excess flux with cloth when cool

Joint Quality: A good solder joint appears shiny and smooth. Dull, grainy solder indicates inadequate heat or movement during cooling—the joint will be weak. Reheat and re-solder if needed.

Brazing (Hard Solder)

Brazing solder composition: Typically 40-50% copper and 50-60% zinc (brass). Melting point 875°C. Creates much stronger joints than soft solder but requires higher heat.

Flux for Brazing: Borax (heated into a glass-like coating) prepares the surface. Apply borax paste to the joint area and heat until it melts into a clear glass coating.

Brazing Process: 1. Heat the joint to 900-950°C (bright red color) 2. Apply brazing solder as a rod or wire to the hot joint 3. Solder flows into the joint gap via capillary action 4. Allow to cool without disturbance 5. Clean excess flux by cooling then soaking in water

Joint Strength: Brazed joints are nearly as strong as the base metal, making them suitable for structural applications. Brazed pipes and vessels can hold pressure.

Silver Brazing (Advanced)

Composition: 40-80% silver with copper and other metals. Extremely strong joints at 600-900°C depending on alloy. Silver solder is expensive but creates very durable joints.

Uses: Precision instrument work, jewelry, and high-reliability repairs. In post-industrial survival, salvaging silver solder from electronic equipment or jewelry provides limited supply.

Non-Ferrous Heat Treatment

Heating and cooling metal in controlled ways strengthens, softens, or changes properties.

Annealing

Purpose: Softens hardened metal for further working. As metal is hammered and bent, it becomes hard and brittle. Annealing restores ductility.

MetalAnnealing TempColor at TempCooling Method
Copper400-600°CDull redSlow cool or quench in water
Bronze (8% Tin)600-700°CBright cherry redSlow cool preferred
Brass500-700°CDull to bright redAir cool or quench
Aluminum300-400°CNot visibly coloredAir cool (very slow)

Annealing Process: Heat the metal to the appropriate temperature. At the right temperature, metal is easy to bend and shape. Too hot causes brittleness; too cool wastes effort. Allow slow cooling to avoid re-hardening. For optimal results, repeat anneal-and-work cycles during projects.

Hardening Through Alloying

Copper Alloys: Bronze and brass are harder than pure copper due to alloying. Increasing tin to 12% or zinc to 30% creates harder metal without heating treatment.

Work Hardening: Repeated hammering hardens metal—useful for creating strong tool edges and shapes. Eventually the metal becomes brittle; anneal before continuing.

Quenching

Purpose: Rapid cooling from high temperature can change metal properties. Most useful for hardening steel (covered in iron-working guides) but affects non-ferrous metals too.

Effects on Non-Ferrous Metals: Quenching bronze or brass in water after heating to 700-800°C hardens the metal slightly. Aluminum should never be quenched after annealing—it weakens the metal. Always air-cool aluminum.

Tempering

Purpose: Reduces brittleness after hardening. Heat the hardened metal to moderate temperature (200-400°C depending on metal), then cool slowly. This balances hardness with ductility.

Color as Temperature Guide: As temperature rises, oxidation creates color changes—pale yellow at 200°C, brown at 300°C, blue at 400°C, etc. Visual timing is common without thermometers.

Application: Springs, cutting edges, and tools benefit from tempering. A bronze edge hardened by quenching then tempered at 300°C becomes tough and durable.
