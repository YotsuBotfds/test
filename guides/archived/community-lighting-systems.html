Community Lighting Systems

Oil Lamp Design and Fuel

Oil lamps efficiently convert liquid fuel into light through controlled combustion. Simple construction, local fuel production, and clean operation make oil lamps ideal for indoor and sheltered lighting.

Basic Oil Lamp Principles

An oil lamp works by absorbing fuel into a wick (fabric strip), which draws fuel upward to a flame at the top. As fuel is consumed, capillary action continuously draws more fuel up the wick. The key parameters are wick size, fuel type, and air circulation.

Essential Components:

  • Fuel container: Holds liquid fuel; must be durable and sealable
  • Wick: Absorbs and draws fuel; cotton works best
  • Wick holder: Adjustable support allowing wick height adjustment
  • Chimney (optional): Glass or ceramic cylinder directing heat and improving draft
  • Reflector (optional): Directs light outward

Fuel Types and Production

Tallow (rendered animal fat): Most accessible fuel in agricultural communities. Produces adequate light but smoky and smelly. Burns easily, requires no special equipment.

Whale oil (spermaceti): Premium fuel producing excellent light with minimal smoke. Regional availability only (coastal communities). Extremely bright and clean burning when available.

Plant oils: Pressed from seeds (sunflower, sesame, olive, walnut). Clean burning, varying brightness depending on oil type. Olive oil burns efficiently with minimal smoke.

Rendered fat from rendering process: Byproduct of meat processing. Identical to tallow; abundant in meat-producing regions.

Fish oil: Extracted from fish livers or fish processing waste. Produces good light; distinctive smell. Coastal communities with fish processing can produce abundant fuel.

Oil Lamp Construction

Simple Pottery Lamp (Ancient Design): Small ceramic vessel with pinched spout.

  1. Mold pottery vessel (4-6 inches diameter, 2-3 inches deep)
  2. Create spout by pinching one side of rim to create narrow point
  3. Smooth all surfaces for safety and functionality
  4. Fire to harden ceramic
  5. Fill with oil, place twisted fiber or rush as wick in spout
  6. Light wick—continues burning as long as fuel remains

Metal Oil Lamp: Larger, more refined design using metal construction.

  1. Create fuel reservoir from sheet metal (copper, iron, or recycled metal), riveted or brazed together
  2. Add spout for wick to rest on; create point for flame
  3. Add handle for safe carrying (especially for hand lamps)
  4. Polish interior or add reflective surface to improve light output
  5. Seal seams to prevent fuel leakage

Wick Management and Optimization

Wick quality significantly affects lamp function. Ideal wicks are:

  • Soft, absorbent cotton (twisted or braided)
  • 1/4 to 1/2 inch diameter depending on lamp size
  • Tightly twisted or braided for even fuel distribution
  • Free from knots or discontinuities

Wick Height Adjustment: Proper wick height (about 1/4 inch above fuel line at start) produces optimal light. Too high creates smoking; too low produces poor light. Adjustable wick holders allow height modification during burning.

Wick Trimming: As wick burns, carbon accumulates at tip. Occasional trimming removes carbon, improving light quality and reducing smoke. Trim burned wick tip by pinching off gently or snipping with scissors.

Wick Replacement: Wicks eventually burn down. Replace by removing old wick, inserting new twisted/braided cotton through holder, and lighting.

Safety and Efficiency

Placement: Oil lamps are open flames and require careful placement away from curtains, hair, and flammable materials. Place on stable surfaces at appropriate height to prevent tipping.

Fuel Efficiency: Oil lamps consume fuel continuously when burning. Calculate fuel needs: small lamp burns approximately 1/4 cup of oil per 20-40 hours depending on wick size. Plan fuel production and storage accordingly.

Ventilation: Oil lamps consume oxygen and produce CO₂. In enclosed spaces, ensure adequate ventilation. Excessive indoor lamp use can deplete oxygen in sealed spaces.

Candle Manufacturing at Scale

Candle production converts tallow or wax into portable light sources. Candle manufacturing is labor-intensive but produces products easier to distribute and use than oil lamps.

Tallow Candle Production

Tallow candles are the most accessible candle type—product of meat processing waste. Production is simple and suitable for community scale.

Basic Tallow Candle Process:

  1. Render tallow (melt fat, strain impurities—see animal materials processing guide)
  2. Heat tallow to liquid state (around 150-160°F/66-71°C)
  3. Prepare wicks: twist or braid cotton fibers tightly, creating core of desired candle diameter
  4. Prepare dipping tank: large container wide enough for wick length (24-30 inches for standard candles)
  5. Dip twisted wick repeatedly into liquid tallow, allowing each coat to cool between dips
  6. Continue dipping 20-40 times until candle reaches desired thickness
  7. Final dip in clean tallow provides smooth surface
  8. Suspend finished candles to cool completely before use

Result: rustic tallow candles, inexpensive to produce, adequate light quality, distinctive smell.

Wax Candle Production

Beeswax candles burn cleaner and longer than tallow. Production is similar but requires wax instead of tallow.

Beeswax Candle Process: Identical to tallow dipping (steps 1-8 above) but using melted beeswax instead of tallow. Beeswax is more expensive but superior product: burns longer, produces less smoke, pleasant aroma.

Wax Blending: Pure beeswax is expensive. Blend with tallow or plant wax (if available) to reduce cost while improving quality over pure tallow: 25% beeswax + 75% tallow produces excellent candles at moderate cost.

Dipped Candle Production Optimization

Large-Scale Dipping Setup:

  • Multiple dipping tanks (3-5) at different stages of cooling
  • Initial heating tank: keeps wax at liquid state (150-160°F)
  • Intermediate tanks: allow cooling between dips (cooling to 140-150°F)
  • Final tank: clean wax for finishing (separate from cooling tanks to prevent contamination)

Production Efficiency:

  1. Multiple workers with wicks of different diameters (making candles of different sizes simultaneously)
  2. Rotate workers through tanks in sequence: dip at tank 1, move to tank 2, continue rotation
  3. As worker completes dipping at final tank, wick hangs to cool; next worker takes wick and begins dipping at tank 1
  4. Continuous rotation produces steady stream of finished candles

Production Rate: Experienced team of 3-4 workers can produce 200-400 candles daily using dipping method. Duration depends on desired candle size and thickness.

Candle Molding Alternative

Molded Candles: Faster production for uniform candles.

  1. Create molds from ceramic or metal: cylindrical containers of desired candle diameter
  2. Secure wick down center of mold (suspend from top or weight at bottom)
  3. Pour melted wax into mold around wick
  4. Allow complete cooling (12-24 hours depending on candle size)
  5. Pop finished candle out of mold
  6. Trim wick to appropriate length (1/4 inch)

Molded candles are faster to produce but create uniform product less suited to varied uses. Combination approach (dipped for specialty, molded for bulk production) optimizes output.

Candle Storage and Uses

Storage: Candles stored in cool, dry location maintain quality indefinitely. Protect from direct sun (prevents warping). Stack candles carefully to prevent damage.

Usage: Candles are ideal for portable lighting (can be carried), functional even in slight wind/draft (unlike oil lamps), and easily distributed to community members. Single candle provides adequate light for reading or detail work in small space.

Special Purposes: Candles can be scented (by adding dried herbs during melting) for functional benefits (insect repellent, antimicrobial). Emergency candles (tall, durable) burn for extended periods and can be distributed as emergency supplies.

Street Lighting Systems

Community streets require lighting for safe nighttime navigation, social gathering, and security. Street lighting systems create visible infrastructure using portable lamps and fixed installations.

Street Lamp Design

Hanging Lamp System: Lamps suspended from structures at strategic locations along streets.

  • Support structure: Wooden poles, mounted on buildings, or spanning across streets
  • Lamp fixture: Oil lamp or large candle in protective housing
  • Reflector: Metal or polished material backing lamp to direct light downward and outward
  • Cover: Protective housing (wood frame, metal grating) protecting lamp from wind and weather

Hanging Lamp Construction:

  1. Craft lamp fixture: iron or brass container with oil lamp inside
  2. Add reflector behind lamp (metal sheet, polished to high shine)
  3. Create protective frame: wooden or iron frame around lamp, leaving open bottom for light output
  4. Suspend from overhead structure using rope or chain
  5. Position lamps 10-15 feet high for street lighting coverage

Lamp Spacing and Coverage

Adequate street lighting requires planned spacing for safety without wasting fuel.

Fuel Supply and Maintenance

Lamp Fuel Schedule: Oil lamps burn 1/4 cup fuel per 20-40 hours. Street lamp burning 8-10 hours nightly consumes approximately 1 pint weekly. Community-wide street lighting with 20 lamps requires about 10 gallons weekly—200 gallons monthly.

Fuel Production Planning: Establish tallow rendering or oil production to support street lighting. Community butchering provides tallow; agricultural areas produce seed oils. Plan sufficient production capacity.

Maintenance Schedule:

  • Daily (evening): Refill lamps, check wicks, light lamps
  • Every 3 days: Trim wicks, clean reflectors
  • Weekly: Clean lamp glass/openings, check structural integrity
  • Monthly: Inspect for damage, repair/replace as needed

Designate lamp keeper or crew responsible for maintenance. Reliable street lighting requires consistent attention.

Lighting Safety and Protocols

Ignition Method: Use long tapers or extended lighters to safely light elevated lamps. Design lamp placement allowing safe access from ground or ladder.

Fire Safety: Street lamps are open flames. Ensure adequate distance from buildings and flammable materials. Inspect structures regularly for fire hazards.

Security: Street lighting improves safety and security. Well-lit areas deter crime and accidents. Coordinate lamp placement with community security priorities.

Reflector Design for Light Amplification

Reflectors multiply light output by directing all light outward rather than allowing it to scatter or dissipate. Proper reflector design significantly improves lighting efficiency.

Reflector Geometry

Reflector shape determines light distribution. Three primary designs serve different purposes:

  • Parabolic reflector: Concentrates light into beam (best for distance lighting)
  • Hemispherical reflector: Distributes light broadly (best for area lighting)
  • Flat reflector: Directs light to one side (best for directional lighting)

Parabolic Design: Light source positioned at focal point of parabolic surface concentrates all light into forward beam. This design maximizes light reach and is ideal for beacons or long-distance lighting.

Hemispherical Design: Light source positioned at center of hemispherical surface. Reflects light in all directions, providing broad area lighting. Ideal for street lamps and gathering areas.

Flat Design: Reflector surface behind lamp directs light outward and downward. Simple to construct, effective for wall-mounted or shelf lamps.

Reflective Material Selection

Material Quality Determines Efficiency:

  • Polished metal (copper, brass): 85-95% reflectivity; highest quality, durable
  • Aluminum or tin plate: 80-90% reflectivity; good quality, readily available
  • Whitewashed surface: 60-80% reflectivity; adequate, simple to maintain
  • Burnished stone: 50-70% reflectivity; moderate, suitable for rough applications

Reflector efficiency decreases over time (dust, oxidation, weathering). Regular cleaning and maintenance preserve reflectivity.

Reflector Construction Methods

Metal Reflector (Best Quality):

  1. Create mold or form (wood, clay, or metal shape) in desired parabolic/hemispherical shape
  2. Hammer thin metal sheet over form, creating reflective surface
  3. Polish metal surface to high shine (use fine abrasive, grease for polishing)
  4. Mount lamp fixture at focal point or center of reflector

Whitewashed Reflector (Simplest):

  1. Create base structure from clay or stone (molded or carved into reflective shape)
  2. Apply lime plaster or whitewash (chalk and water mixture) to surface
  3. Allow to dry; reapply if necessary to maximize whiteness
  4. Mount lamp in appropriate position

Composite Reflector (Balance of Quality and Complexity):

  1. Create base structure from wood or clay
  2. Attach metal sheet (tin, copper, or aluminum) to base
  3. Polish metal surface for reflectivity
  4. Secure lamp fixture

Reflector Maintenance

Cleaning Schedule: Reflector efficiency drops rapidly with dust/soot accumulation. Clean weekly to maintain effectiveness.

Cleaning Methods:

  • Metal reflectors: Soft cloth with mild abrasive (fine sand, wood ash) and oil; rub gently to remove oxidation and dirt
  • Whitewashed reflectors: Soft cloth with water, or light scrubbing with mild abrasive; reapply whitewash as needed

Oxidation Management: Metal reflectors oxidize (tarnish) over time. Prevent by coating with thin oil layer or varnish; however, this may reduce maximum reflectivity slightly. Trade-off between maintenance frequency and maximum efficiency.

Torch and Lantern Construction

Portable lighting allows movement through dark areas without fixed infrastructure. Torches and lanterns serve for emergency evacuation, nighttime work, and exploration of dark spaces.

Torch Construction

Simple handheld torches are essential emergency lighting.

Basic Torch Design:

  1. Create torch head: bundle dried plant material (rush, straw, or bark fibers) into tight bundle, 2-4 inches diameter
  2. Wrap bundle with twine or cloth strips, binding tightly
  3. Soak bundle in melted tallow or oil for 30 minutes, allowing complete saturation
  4. Allow to cool and dry (cloth covering prevents dripping)
  5. Create handle: attach torch head to wooden pole (4-6 feet long) using twine or metal bands

Refined Torch (Metal-Bound):

  1. Create torch head as above
  2. Wrap metal band (iron, copper) tightly around torch head, securing in place
  3. Metal band prevents torch head from loosening and provides structural support
  4. Create handle and attach to metal band

Torch Duration and Fuel: Torch burns 1-2 hours depending on size and fuel type. Burning rate: approximately 1 ounce of fuel per hour. Plan torch quantity based on need (emergency evacuation of 200 people requires 50-100 torches to allow safe group movement).

Lantern Construction

Enclosed lanterns protect flame from wind and prevent injury from open flame.

Simple Wooden Lantern:

  1. Create frame: wooden strips arranged in rectangular or cylindrical box shape (6-8 inches cube or taller)
  2. Add openings: leave top open for oil lamp or candle placement
  3. Create door or cover: allow safe insertion and removal of light source
  4. Ventilation: small holes in top allow smoke escape without introducing wind
  5. Interior finish: whitewash or light paint to improve reflectivity
  6. Mount candle or oil lamp securely in center of lantern

Metal Lantern (Superior):

  1. Create frame from iron or brass strips, welded or riveted together
  2. Add glass or mica panels (if available) for viewing while protecting flame
  3. Create hinged door allowing lamp access
  4. Add ventilation holes in top
  5. Secure lamp fixture internally; mount oil lamp or candle securely

Lantern Uses and Features

Advantages Over Open Torches:

  • Wind protection (flame less likely to extinguish in breeze)
  • Rain protection (flame protected from water)
  • Safety (enclosed flame reduces burn risk, protects surroundings)
  • Efficiency (reflective interior amplifies light)

Specialized Lanterns:

  • Colored glass lanterns: Colored or red-tinted glass reduces light output but allows dark-adapted vision; useful for night work requiring sight adjustment
  • Directional lanterns: Reflector inside creates directional beam; useful for searching or illuminating specific areas
  • Hooded lanterns: Open on one side with hood and reflector directing light in one direction; used for navigation and emergency signaling

Gas Lighting from Biogas

Biogas (methane from anaerobic decomposition) can fuel gas lamps, providing lighting from waste decomposition. While less bright than oil or candles, gas lighting produces excellent light without fuel consumption of solid light sources.

Biogas Production for Lighting

See Industrial Fermentation guide for detailed biogas production. Key points for lighting application:

  • Biogas from anaerobic digestion is approximately 60% methane (burnable gas) and 40% CO₂
  • Biogas burns with blue flame, producing adequate heat and light
  • Gas output from digester: approximately 0.5-1 cubic foot per pound of organic matter digested
  • Storage in bags or sealed containers allows fuel accumulation and flexible use

Gas Lamp Design

Simple Gas Lamp:

  1. Create burner: small metal tube (1/4 to 1/2 inch diameter) with small holes or slit allowing gas escape and combustion
  2. Design gas inlet allowing controlled flow rate (needle valve or simple restriction)
  3. Position burner in lamp enclosure with reflector
  4. Connect gas supply line (cloth tube, plant fiber tube, or hollow wooden pipe) to biogas storage
  5. Light burner at opening; adjust flow for desired flame size

Mantle-Style Lamp (Advanced): Use mesh mantle (calcium nitrate treated cloth) positioned above gas burner. When heated by flame, mantle becomes incandescent and produces bright light (similar to modern camping lanterns). Requires knowledge of mantle creation or salvage of pre-collapse mantles.

Gas Lamp Advantages and Challenges

Advantages:

  • No wicks to trim or maintain
  • Clean burning (minimal smoke or smell with proper adjustments)
  • Bright light from small burner
  • Fuel source utilizes waste material (no opportunity cost)

Challenges:

  • Requires biogas production infrastructure (anaerobic digester)
  • Gas pressure/storage needs careful management
  • Safety considerations (combustible gas, potential leaks)
  • Requires tubing or piping infrastructure for fuel delivery

Gas lighting is best suited for communities with established biogas production and technical expertise. Simpler lighting methods (oil, candles) are more accessible initially.

Solar-Charged Salvaged LED Systems

In communities with access to pre-collapse salvage (solar panels, LED lights, batteries), salvage-based lighting provides modern efficiency without ongoing fuel production.

Salvage-Based Systems Caveat: These systems depend on salvaged pre-collapse technology. Once salvage is exhausted or components fail, replacement becomes impossible without manufacturing capability. Communities should not rely solely on salvage systems but should develop parallel renewable fuel-based lighting (oil, candles, biogas) for long-term resilience.

Solar Panel and Battery System

Basic Setup:

  1. Salvage solar panels (5-20 watt capacity typical)
  2. Salvage rechargeable batteries (lithium or lead-acid)
  3. Create charging controller: simple device regulating power flow from panels to batteries (prevents overcharging)
  4. Connect LED light fixtures to battery

System Sizing:

  • Solar panel capacity should match daily energy needs plus cloudy-day reserves
  • Battery capacity should allow 2-3 days operation without sunlight
  • LED consumption: typical LED lamp uses 1-5 watts compared to 15-40 watts for oil lamp equivalent brightness

LED Light Fixtures

Fixture Types:

  • Direct LED lamps: Single or multiple LEDs in simple fixture, powered by battery
  • Reflector-enhanced fixtures: LEDs positioned in reflectors for enhanced light output
  • Multi-LED assemblies: Multiple LEDs creating higher total brightness

Advantages of LED: Extreme efficiency (0.5-2 watts produces equivalent light to 10-50 watt incandescent), extended lifespan (10,000+ hours), cold operation (minimal heat output).

System Maintenance and Longevity

Expected Component Lifespan:

  • Solar panels: 20-30 years with proper care
  • LED lights: 10,000-50,000 hours (5-15 years at 8 hours daily use)
  • Batteries: 5-10 years (lithium); 3-5 years (lead-acid)
  • Charging controllers: 5-10 years (simple controllers most reliable)

Maintenance:

  • Keep solar panels clean (dust, leaf litter reduce efficiency)
  • Monitor battery charge level; avoid complete discharge (shortens lifespan)
  • Replace LED lights when brightness decreases noticeably
  • Protect from weather exposure (creates corrosion and water damage)

Sustainable System Design

Communities should not exclusively rely on salvage systems. Optimal approach combines salvage-based and renewable fuel-based systems:

  • High-value locations: Critical areas (emergency response centers, medical facilities) use salvage LED systems to preserve their function
  • General community lighting: Oil lamps, candles, and other renewable fuel systems provide baseline lighting
  • Backup systems: Torches and lanterns provide emergency lighting independent of fuel supply or salvage inventory

This diversified approach ensures lighting continues even as salvage resources are exhausted and renewable systems mature to replace them.
