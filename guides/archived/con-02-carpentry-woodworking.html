Historical Woodworking Context

Medieval Guild Tradition (1000-1700 CE)

Woodworking guilds maintained strict apprenticeship systems: 7 years apprentice, 4-7 years journeyman, then master craftsman. Knowledge transferred orally through hands-on training. Guild members created structures lasting 400+ years (many medieval buildings still standing). Key principle: quality materials, superior joinery, and patient craftsmanship create longevity. Post-collapse, this guild model likely remerges (apprenticeship critical to knowledge transfer). Medieval carpenters could work exclusively with hand tools and natural fasteners, producing buildings of stunning durability. Westminster Abbey, built 1245-1517, still stands with original timber framing intact.

Tool Evolution (1800-2000)

Power tools (saws, sanders) dramatically increased speed but reduced skill requirements. Modern apprentices often rely on power tools and can't hand-tool effectively. Pre-electric woodworking required significant hand skill. Historical woodworkers produced superior results with hand tools due to intimate material understanding. Post-collapse, hand-tool mastery becomes essential (no electricity). The transition from hand tools to power occurred over merely 150 years; reversing this transition requires dedicated practice and mentorship.

Industrial Revolution Impact

Mass-produced furniture (2000s onwards) eliminated traditional joinery in favor of cheap fasteners (screws, nails) and poor-quality plywood. Furniture designed for 5-10 year lifespan. Historical mortise-tenon joinery designed for 100+ year lifespan. Post-collapse survival requires returning to traditional durable methods. Understanding why traditional methods prevailed teaches us that speed and ease are not the ultimate goals—longevity and reliability are.

Timber Framing in Early America

Settlers arriving in 1600s-1700s brought medieval timber-framing knowledge. Post-and-beam construction created homes lasting 300+ years with minimal maintenance. Colonial carpenters produced entire buildings with hand tools: pit saws, hand planes, chisels, and augers. Master carpenters commanded high status and wages. Knowledge of wood properties, joinery angles, and timber sizing was closely guarded. Traditional apprenticeships ensured knowledge transfer across generations.

Wood Identification & Properties

Identifying Trees and Wood Types

Successful woodworking begins with correct wood identification. Different species have vastly different properties—using wrong wood for application causes failure. Key identification features: bark appearance, leaf shape (if tree still living), wood color (heartwood vs sapwood), grain pattern, density, smell when cut.

Major Hardwood Species

Oak (Red/White): Very dense (0.75 specific gravity), rot-resistant heartwood, distinctive ray fleck pattern. Excellent structural timber. Heavy (makes transport difficult). Difficult to cut by hand (requires sharp tools). Color: light tan to reddish-brown. Smell: characteristic tannic odor. Best uses: posts, beams, furniture frames that must last centuries.

Maple: Extremely hard (0.65 sg), dense, light color. Excellent tool handles and wear surfaces (floors). Difficult to cut. Machines better than cuts by hand. Best uses: tool handles (shock absorption), high-wear furniture surfaces. Cost: expensive due to scarcity in survival situations.

Ash: High strength (0.65 sg), flexible when bent, light tan color. Excellent for tool handles. Superior shock absorption. Used historically for axe handles, oar blades. Best uses: handles for tools, bent-wood applications, situations requiring flexibility and strength.

Walnut: Dark brown, high rot resistance, excellent finishing properties. Machine-work wood (cuts cleanly). Relatively soft (0.55 sg) compared to oak. Expensive. Best uses: fine furniture, visible surfaces where appearance matters.

Major Softwood Species

Pine: Low density (0.35-0.50 sg), easy to work, lightweight. Requires protection from rot (heartwood has some resistance, sapwood none). Fast-growing (readily available post-collapse from old-growth forests being recycled). Excellent for general framing, furniture. Takes glue well. Grain visible, takes stain evenly. Best uses: general building, framing, painted furniture.

Spruce: Very light (0.35 sg), straight grain, low rot resistance. Excellent for large-span beams (high strength-to-weight ratio). Used historically for masts on ships. Best uses: joists, rafters, applications where lightweight strength needed.

Fir: Medium density (0.48 sg), very straight grain, low rot resistance. Strong in bending. Good for beams and joists. More decay-resistant than spruce/pine but inferior to oak. Best uses: structural framing, general building.

Cedar/Redwood: Low density, high natural rot resistance (heartwood contains oils). Excellent for exterior applications. Softer than pine (easier cutting). Attractive appearance. Best uses: exterior siding, outdoor furniture, applications exposed to weather.

Wood Density and Strength Relationships

Density directly correlates with strength: higher density means stronger, more durable, harder to work. Softwoods (gymnosperm trees) have lower density; hardwoods (angiosperm trees) have higher density. Paradoxically, "hardwood" and "softwood" are botanical terms, not hardness measures—balsa is technically a hardwood but very soft. When choosing materials: use dense woods for structural members and tool handles; use lighter woods for general framing and furniture where weight matters.

Seasoning & Drying Methods

Why Seasoning Matters

Freshly cut wood (green wood) contains 50-100% moisture by weight. Green wood is heavy, weak, and will shrink unpredictably as it dries. Uncontrolled drying causes checking (surface cracks), warping, and twisting. Properly seasoned lumber (12-15% moisture content) is stable, strong, and predictable. Seasoning takes months to years—planning ahead is critical in survival situations.

Air Drying Methods

Stack drying: Standard method. Stack lumber in loosely organized piles with 2cm spacers between pieces allowing air circulation. Protect from direct rain but allow air flow. Stack on raised base (off ground, prevents moisture absorption from soil). Orientation: stack with grain running parallel for most stable drying. Softwood dries 6-12 weeks; hardwood requires 6-18 months depending on thickness. Average drying rate: 2-3cm thickness per year for hardwoods.

Kiln drying (traditional method): Build shed with good air circulation but rain protection. Hang lumber vertically or stack with wide spacers. Generate heat using fireplace or sun to accelerate drying (not directly heating—ambient heat only). Speeds drying 2-3x compared to open-air drying. Can achieve 12-15% moisture in 6-12 weeks for softwoods, 4-6 months for hardwoods.

Bark removal timing: Remove bark within 2-4 weeks of felling (bark holds moisture, prevents drying). After this window, bark must be soaked or split off manually—more labor intensive. Bark-off wood dries faster but is more prone to checking. Bark-on wood dries slowly but evenly.

Moisture Detection Methods

Simple weight test: Green wood is 50% heavier than dry wood. Compare sample weight to historical data for wood species. Sample loss of 10-15 lbs per board-foot indicates adequate drying.

Visual inspection: Fully dried wood shows: no surface moisture, smooth grain (raised grain settles), no smell of rot or mold. Moisture-laden wood appears wet on cut surfaces, smells earthy, grain appears raised.

Knife test: Slice thin shaving from wood. Green wood shaving is wet, flexible. Dry wood shaving is crisp, breaks easily.

Hand Tool Selection & Maintenance

In survival situations, you must make tools from natural materials. Understanding both commercially available and improvised tools ensures you can work in any scenario. Tool selection directly impacts efficiency and safety.

Essential Tools & Alternatives

  • Saw (critical): Commercial hand saw superior but improvised bone/stone saw functional on softwood. Bone saws require 20% more effort but work. Saw type determines technique: Western saws cut on push stroke; Japanese saws cut on pull stroke. Two-person pit saw (7-foot length) needed for large logs.
  • Axe: Hafted stone head or bone wedge effective for splitting. Modern axes superior for speed, but stone tools sufficient. Weight matters: heavier axe (2kg+) requires less force but demands perfect technique (wrong angle causes injury).
  • Chisels (critical for joinery): Sharpened bone superior to stone (doesn't chip). Stone chisels 0.5cm diameter sufficient for mortise work. Bone chisels 1cm diameter better for control. Hand-forged steel chisels (if available) far superior to improvised alternatives.
  • Hammer/Mallet: Rock with flat face sufficient. Wooden mallets (hardwood head on wooden handle) better (less damaging to wood). Weight: 0.5-1kg sufficient for chisel work.
  • Plane (optional but valuable): Sharpened bone blade set in wooden frame works but slowly. Metal planes far superior. Key: blade must be sharp—dull planes frustrating and dangerous (require excessive force).
  • Clamps: Rope tension clamps (twisted rope around wood with stick for tightening) sufficient for most work. Wooden screw clamps (5cm diameter wooden screw) provide mechanical advantage. Bar clamps (wooden beam with tightening mechanism) useful for larger assemblies.

Making a Versatile Bone Adze

  1. Locate large animal bone (cow femur ideal, 8-10cm diameter)
  2. Strike to create sharp edge perpendicular to long axis (creates tool face)
  3. Create hafting notch by chipping edges (groove for lashing)
  4. Use sinew or plant fiber cordage to lash bone to hardwood handle (15-20cm length)
  5. Test edge sharpness before heavy use (light touch should peel bark)
  6. Resharpen by careful bone-on-stone work (maintain perpendicular edge)
  7. Tool ready for carving, shaping, and general woodwork

Tool Maintenance Schedule

Daily: Wipe tools clean, store in dry location (rust prevention). Check for damage. Weekly: Sharpen chisels and saw (dull tools=dangerous tools). Test blade sharpness on arm hair (if doesn't cut easily, sharpen more). Monthly: Oil wooden handles (linseed oil prevents cracking). Check hafting (lashing) on stone tools for wear. Replace worn cordage immediately. Quarterly: Deep maintenance—check entire tool for damage, restore surfaces, replace broken components. Tool maintenance consumes 20-30% of woodworking time; this is not wasted time—it's essential to safety and efficiency.

Hand Tool Techniques & Fundamentals

Saw Technique Fundamentals

Start with light pressure allowing saw weight to do work. Teeth do cutting on the push stroke (Western saws) or pull stroke (Japanese saws). Maintain straight cut by watching both sides of blade. Let saw do the work; excessive pressure forces blade and causes binding. Sharp saw essential (dull saw requires excessive force and accuracy). Hold saw at 45-degree angle to wood surface for crosscutting; shallow angle (20 degrees) for ripping along grain. Maintain consistent stroke rhythm—speed develops naturally with practice.

Chiseling Technique

Keep chisel sharp (dull chisel bounces and skips). Hold at 15-20 degree angle to wood surface. Strike with mallet using controlled force—light, repeated blows better than heavy single strike. Work from both sides toward center to prevent tearout on exit side. Grain direction critical: chisel down-slope of grain. Remove material in 5-10mm layers. Test fit regularly; small adjustments better than large corrections. Never force chisel into wood—if resistant, work from other side or reduce blade depth.

Planing Technique & Hand Plane Setup

Shaving thickness: adjust blade to take 0.5-1mm shaving (visible light through shaving when held up). Blade setting critical—too deep creates binding and tearout; too shallow removes no material. Push plane with steady pressure, release pressure at end of stroke to prevent blade from catching wood end. Plane with grain (downslope of fibers). Cross-grain planing possible but requires care and sharp blade. Sole of plane must be flat and smooth (high spots prevent even shaving). Test sole regularly using straight edge.

Measuring and Marking Fundamentals

Accuracy in layout prevents errors in cutting. Use knife (not pencil) for marking—knife cuts fibers cleanly, pencil creates broad indistinct lines. Create story stick (piece of wood marked with all project dimensions) for repeated layout. Double-check all dimensions before cutting. Mark on waste side of line where possible (allows small errors without ruining work). Use square to mark perpendicular lines. Use bevel gauge to copy angles from existing work.

Fundamental Joinery: Mortise & Tenon

Mortise and tenon joints are strongest traditional wood-to-wood connections. Widely used in furniture, doors, windows, and frames. Foundation of all serious woodworking.

Basic Mortise & Tenon Design Principles

Mortise: Rectangular hole in receiving wood. Standard dimensions: depth 2/3 of wood thickness; width 1/3 of wood width; length 2.5-3 times width. Depth insufficient creates weak joint; too-deep mortise weakens receiving wood. Tenon: Projecting piece from joining wood, fitting into mortise. Must be slightly smaller than mortise (0.5mm tolerance) for tight fit when glued. Undersized tenon (loose fit) cannot be tightened; oversized tenon (tight fit) cannot be removed for adjustments.

Cutting Mortise by Hand

  1. Mark mortise layout on wood using knife—cuts fibers cleanly, enables precise chiseling
  2. Create small pilot hole at mortise center using bone drill or stone chisel
  3. Use sharp chisel to remove wood in layers (5-10mm depth per pass)
  4. Work from both sides toward center to avoid tearout at far side
  5. Cut perpendicular to wood surface; do not angle chisel (creates weak joint)
  6. Check fit with tenon test piece; adjust as needed (chiseling removes material gradually)
  7. Final fit should require light hammer taps to seat fully (indicates good glue fit)

Cutting Tenon by Hand

  1. Mark tenon profile on four sides using knife and square
  2. Use saw to cut long-grain sides (create vertical walls of tenon)
  3. Use chisel to shape cross-grain shoulders where tenon meets body wood
  4. Test fit in mortise regularly; remove small amounts of material
  5. Final dimension: tenon fully enters mortise with light resistance
  6. Ensure grain direction on tenon faces matches body wood (reduces splitting)

Layout Precision Critical for Success

Mortise and tenon success requires accurate layout. Use sharpened bone or hardwood marking gauge. Mark mortise and tenon dimensions on all pieces before cutting any. Double-check all dimensions before committing cuts (errors in layout cannot be corrected). Mark grain direction on all pieces (helps coordinate grain orientation in final assembly). Create test joint first using scrap wood identical to finished piece—reveals technique issues before ruining material.

Joinery Techniques Comparison

Mortise-and-Tenon: The Gold Standard

Mortise-and-tenon joints provide maximum strength (90%+ of beam strength) through combined gluing and mechanical fit. Used in structural timber framing, fine furniture, and any application requiring ultimate durability. Cutting requires significant time (1-2 hours per joint). Strength: 2000+ lbs per square inch of glue area. Primary disadvantage: high labor investment per joint.

Dovetail Joints: Mechanical Interlocking

Dovetail joints provide mechanical locking through angled, interlocking pins and tails. Strength 95%+ of wood strength (second only to mortise-tenon). Primarily used in drawer construction and fine boxes where pulling stress is high. Advantage: joint remains solid even without glue (mechanical lock prevents separation). Disadvantage: very difficult to cut, requires precision layout and careful handwork. Learning curve steep.

Lap Joints: Simplicity and Speed

Lap joints (half-lap, full lap) created by overlapping wood and removing material so pieces lie flush. Simple to lay out and cut (30 minutes per joint). Strength moderate (70% of beam strength). Adequate for non-critical connections, temporary structures. T-lap joints connect perpendicular members (T-shaped intersection). Full-lap joints similar strength to half-lap but more material removal.

Tongue-and-Groove: Edge Joining

Tongue-and-groove joints connect boards edge-to-edge (prevents cupping in wide boards). Strength adequate (75% for glued joints, 50% for unglued). Groove cut into one board, tongue cut on other. Labor moderate. Commonly used for wide panels (floors, tabletops). Allows wood movement along grain direction.

Box Joints: Geometric Alternative

Box joints (also called finger joints) use rectangular protrusions instead of angled dovetails. Easier to cut than dovetails (particularly with simple jigs). Strength slightly less than dovetails but superior to butt joints. Commonly used in simple boxes and light furniture. Can be machine-made efficiently.

Advanced Joinery: Dovetail Joints

Dovetail joints provide superior strength and mechanical interlocking. Traditionally used in drawers and boxes where pulling stress is high.

Dovetail Types & Applications

Through dovetails: Tails (protruding pieces) visible on both sides. Strongest (mechanical lock prevents separation). Typical angle: 8-degree slope (1:6 ratio in softwood, 1:8 in hardwood). Used in structural applications and fine furniture where appearance on all sides acceptable. Half-blind dovetails: Tails hidden from one side. Used where appearance matters (drawer fronts, cabinet sides). Requires careful layout and intermediate skill level. Box joints: Simplified version using rectangular protrusions instead of angled. Faster to cut, adequate strength for light boxes, geometric appearance less refined but acceptable for utility boxes.

Layout for Through Dovetails

  1. Measure and mark baselines on all pieces (typically 2-3mm in from edge)
  2. Divide width into equal sections (typical: 3-5 tail sections per side)
  3. Draw angled lines at 8-degree slope from baselines to represent tails
  4. Mark pin areas (receiving wood) by transferring tail pattern using knife
  5. Label all pieces clearly and mark grain direction (critical for strength)
  6. Create sketch showing which pieces are tails and which are pins (easy to confuse)

Cutting Through Dovetails by Hand

  1. Clamp wood vertically in vise with grain vertical (prevents splitting)
  2. Use hand saw to cut tail edges at 8-degree angle
  3. Cut with saw leading on waste side—do not touch scribed line (prevents splintering)
  4. Chop out waste between tails using sharp chisel (work from both sides toward center)
  5. Test fit with pin board (receiving board); adjust if necessary (remove small amounts only)
  6. Mark pin positions by placing tail board on top of pin board and scribing knife along tail edges
  7. Cut pins identically to tails (slightly smaller for glue fit)
  8. Final assembly: tap together with mallet; glue if permanent joint desired

Dovetail Spacing & Proportions

Minimum tail width: 1.5cm for softwood, 1cm for hardwood (avoids splits during cutting). Spacing affects appearance and strength: widely spaced tails (5-8cm) appear coarse but are strongest (fewer tails=less cutting); closely spaced tails (1-2cm) appear refined but require precision and are weaker (more tails=more potential failure points). Standard spacing: 3-4 tails per 30cm width creates balanced appearance and adequate strength.

Timber Frame Construction

Timber framing creates strong structures using massive timber beams joined with traditional joinery. Foundational for buildings that last centuries.

Structural Design Principles

  • Load paths: Design structures so loads travel directly through beams to ground. Avoid complex stress patterns (loads must follow shortest path to foundation).
  • Beam sizing rule of thumb: Depth in inches should equal span in feet divided by 20. Example: 20-foot span requires 12-inch beam minimum depth. Conservative formula; use this for safety margin.
  • Joint strength requirement: Joints must be stronger than adjoining beams (typically 90% of beam strength minimum). Weak joints cause catastrophic collapse; overbuilt joints are acceptable insurance.
  • Redundancy principle: Design so single joint failure does not collapse structure. Dual connections or backup support prevents progressive failure.
  • Timber grading: Select knot-free, straight-grained timber for critical structural members. Knots reduce strength 20-30%. Grain deviation (spiral grain) reduces strength.

Common Timber Frame Joints

Post-to-beam: Mortise and tenon where beam sits in post mortise. Post absorbs bearing load directly. Requires 25% post diameter depth mortise (e.g., 40cm post requires 10cm deep mortise). Tenon should be at least 1/2 post width. Load rating: up to 10 tons per joint in hardwood. Beam-to-beam: Joinery where beams meet at angles. Commonly: half-lap joints (25% material removal from each piece), mortise-tenon combinations (one beam mortised into another), or scarfs (overlapping angled joints for end-to-end connection). Connection hardware: Use wooden pegs (10-12mm diameter hardwood) to lock joints, preventing movement from wood shrinkage or seasonal expansion. Pegs should be driven at slight angle (prevents splitting). Wooden pegs superior to nails (reversible, don't rust, don't weaken wood as much).

Raising Timber Frame Structure

  1. Assemble frame on ground with all joints fitted dry (test assembly without glue)
  2. Test all connections; adjust before final assembly (ground-level adjustments safest)
  3. Disassemble completely
  4. Reassemble at height using gin pole (mechanical advantage lever) or block and tackle system
  5. Insert pegs into holes during assembly to lock frame (pegs driven after structure raised)
  6. Check for square using diagonal measurements (opposite diagonals should be equal)
  7. Brace frame temporarily until roof attaches (temporary bracing critical to worker safety)
  8. Final inspection: check all joints for gaps, all pegs for tightness, entire frame for plumb (vertical)

Furniture Building Basics

Building sturdy furniture ensures comfort in survival situations. Functional designs are far superior to decorative styles in survival contexts. Well-made furniture lasts generations.

Essential Survival Furniture Projects

Sleeping Platform (Bed)

Elevated 60cm off ground using timber frame and rope webbing or wooden slats. Dimensions: 2m long, 1.2m wide minimum. Frame uses 5cm diameter posts, 10cm cross members joined with mortise-tenon joints. Rope webbing (2cm natural fiber rope) stretched across frame at 15cm spacing provides flexible support. Alternative: split log slats laid 5cm apart (more durable than rope). Typical build time: 20-30 hours. Lifetime: 50+ years with maintenance.

Storage Box

Constructed from planks joined with dovetails or mortise-tenon. Dimensions: 60cm x 40cm x 40cm. Hinged lid with stay bar prevents opening too far (prevents finger trapping). Interior shelf divides space for organization. Bottom board sits on batten supports (prevents moisture absorption through ground contact). Typical build time: 15-20 hours. Construction method: sides dovetailed, bottom set in groove (floating panel allows movement), lid framed with mortise-tenon.

Table

Simple design: four square-section legs (6cm x 6cm), cross-braced at 40cm height for strength. Top made from wide planks (glued and doweled together, or mortised into legs). Height: 75-80cm. Bracing provides torsion strength (prevents twisting). Typical build time: 25-35 hours. Construction method: legs mortised into top frame, cross braces mortised into legs at 45-degree angle for maximum strength.

Chair

Practical design: vertical back support (1.2m), angled seat frame (15-degree slope backward), four legs with cross-bracing. Seat surface made from rope webbing (comfortable and durable). Minimum height: 45cm seat to floor. Typical build time: 30-40 hours. Back rest mortised into seat frame, legs splayed (angled outward) for stability.

Wood Movement Compensation

Wood shrinks and swells with moisture changes. Seasonal movement ranges from 3-8% along grain, 0.5-1% across grain. Account for movement in wide boards: allow 5mm per 30cm width for seasonal movement. Use floating panels (not fully glued) and slots for screw holes (not centered in hole—allows movement). Never glue across grain on wide panels (causes splitting). Frame-and-panel construction (frame mortised/tenoned, panels floating in grooves) standard solution for large surfaces.

Wood Finishing & Preservation

Natural Finishes for Survival Situations

Oil finish (linseed, walnut, tung oil): Traditional protection method. Oil penetrates wood, enhances grain, provides water resistance. Apply multiple coats (3-5 thin coats better than one thick coat). Allow drying between coats (24 hours minimum). Reapply yearly for furniture in high-traffic areas. Disadvantages: slow drying (requires patience), darkens wood color, limited water protection. Advantages: natural appearance, easily repairable, sustainable.

Wax finish: Beeswax or plant-based wax buffed onto oiled wood creates protective layer. Labor-intensive (requires hand buffing) but creates beautiful finish. Provides moderate water resistance. Reapply annually. Primarily decorative finish, not for high-use surfaces.

Shellac (historical): Lac resin dissolved in alcohol. Creates hard finish protective against moisture and abrasion. Labor-intensive application (multiple thin coats). Can be damaged by heat and water. Historically used on fine furniture. Repairable if damaged (resand and recoat). Requires alcohol solvent (obtain from plants post-collapse).

Paint finish: Protect wood from weather and UV damage. Paint hides wood grain but provides superior weathering. For exterior surfaces: apply primer, then 2 coats paint. For interior: single coat adequate if wood sealed first. Paint must be compatible with wood (lime wash or plant-based paint for survival situations).

Rot Prevention Strategies

Design for drainage: Prevent water accumulation. Sloping surfaces shed water. Elevate structures above ground (prevent moisture wicking from soil). Ventilation under and within structures prevents moisture accumulation. Material selection: Use naturally rot-resistant wood (heartwood of cedar, oak, redwood). Avoid sapwood (no rot resistance). Heartwood contains natural oils/chemicals preventing decay. Finish application: Oil finishes provide some protection but not complete. Paint provides superior weathering. Combine paint with good design (sloping, elevated, ventilated) for maximum protection. Maintenance: Inspect regularly for rot signs (soft wood, discoloration, fungal growth). Remove affected wood immediately before decay spreads. Reapply finish every 3-5 years.

Jigs & Fixtures for Repeatability

Why Jigs Matter

Once mastering basic hand-tool techniques, efficiency improves by creating jigs (simple fixtures that position wood and tools precisely). Jigs reduce layout time, improve accuracy, and enable consistent reproduction. Critical for community production.

Simple Mortise Jig

Wooden frame with two vertical posts creating a guide for chisel. Wood positioned in jig at exact height. Chisel guided by posts to cut straight mortise. Jig speeds up mortise cutting 30-50% after initial setup time. Can be built in 2-3 hours from scrap wood.

Tenon Sizing Block

Simple block of wood drilled to exact mortise dimensions. Used to test tenon fit during cutting. Reduces fitting errors. Can be built in 1 hour.

Dovetail Angle Guide

Simple triangle cut at 8-degree angle (1:6 ratio). Used as guide for drawing dovetail angles. Eliminates measurement errors. Improves layout consistency. Can be built in 30 minutes.

Sawing Guide

Simple fence or groove that guides saw straight. Reduces wandering cuts (crooked cuts). Improves accuracy for ripping. Can be as simple as wooden board clamped parallel to cutting line.

Scaling to Production

Single jig saves 15-30 minutes per piece. Community production with 10 pieces per day saves 2.5-5 hours daily (significant productivity gain). Invest time in jig creation early; returns compound over time.

Sharpening Techniques & Tool Care

Sharpening Fundamentals

Sharp tools are safer, more efficient, and produce better results. Dull tools require 3-5x more force, slip unexpectedly, and cause accidents. Maintenance is not optional—it's essential safety.

Sharpening Chisels and Plane Blades

Method 1: Oil stone (fine abrasive stone with oil lubricant): Rub blade at consistent angle (25-30 degrees) back-and-forth on stone. Use light pressure. Work from coarse stone to fine stone progressively. Stop when blade passes arm hair test (cuts arm hair cleanly when drawn across). Method 2: Water stone (fine abrasive stone with water): Similar to oil stone but uses water instead of oil. Faster cutting than oil stone. Stones require flattening periodically (sharp sand or flattening plate). Method 3: Hand stropping (polishing on leather): Final stage after stone sharpening. Leather strap charged with fine polishing compound. Pass blade edge backwards (toward spine) to polish. Creates finest edge.

Saw Sharpening

Hand saw maintenance: Saws become dull faster than chisels (teeth wear from frequent cutting). Filing teeth requires precision: each tooth must be filed at exact angle and depth. Specialized saw file (triangular or tapered) required. Setting teeth (angling teeth alternately left-right) must be maintained during filing. First sharpen file teeth to uniform depth, then set teeth for correct spacing.

Tool Care Schedule

After use (daily): Wipe blade clean, dry completely. Store in dry location (prevent rust). Weekly: Sharpen tools showing dull symptoms (requires increasing force, won't cut arm hair). Don't wait until impossibly dull. Monthly: Deep clean—remove oxidation with fine steel wool, oil metal surfaces. Quarterly: Inspect all tools for damage, replace worn handles, restore surfaces.

Failure Modes: Wood Movement, Joint Failure, Rot

Wood Movement and Seasonal Changes

Problem: Wood shrinks across grain in winter (low humidity); swells in summer (high humidity). Grain direction determines movement: 0.5-1% along grain, 5-8% across grain. Width change in 30cm board: 1-2cm seasonally. Consequences: (1) Glued joints across grain split as wood shrinks. (2) Frames become loose seasonally. (3) Floating panels warp if constrained. (4) Paint cracks on exterior surfaces. Prevention: (1) Never glue wood across grain on wide boards. (2) Use floating panels (frame-and-panel construction). (3) Slot screw holes allowing wood movement. (4) Design flexible joints (mortise-tenon allows slight movement). (5) Apply finish protecting wood from moisture extremes. (6) Maintain stable indoor moisture (if possible).

Joint Failure Modes

Gap formation: Joints cut too loosely, or wood shrinks leaving gaps. Consequences: loads transfer improperly, joint can collapse under high stress. Prevention: test-fit joints to ensure proper fit, account for wood shrinkage in tropical environments (higher humidity). Shear failure: Joint experiences sideways sliding force greater than glue/wood strength. Consequences: joint separates despite glue. Prevention: design joints so loads transfer primarily through wood fibers (mortise-tenon superior to butt joints). Splitting: Tenon or peg hole too large, wood fibers tear in grain direction. Consequences: joint becomes loose, structural failure. Prevention: mortise and tenon properly proportioned, peg holes sized conservatively, work carefully to avoid oversizing holes. Rot: Fungal decay at joint (moisture + warmth + darkness). Consequences: wood becomes soft, joint loses all strength. Prevention: keep joints dry (good finish, ventilation, slope for drainage), use rot-resistant wood species, inspect regularly.

Rot and Decay

Wet rot (fungal): Affects high-moisture wood (over 20% moisture content). Soft rot most common. Dark discoloration, soft wood when poked. Found in damp areas (below grade, around gutters). Dry rot (fungal): Despite name, affects wood around 15% moisture. Fungal decay continues drying wood further. Serious because progresses inside wood. Prevention: (1) Design structures shedding water, (2) Ventilate spaces preventing moisture accumulation, (3) Use rot-resistant wood, (4) Apply finishes protecting from moisture, (5) Keep wood dry—most rot prevention is avoiding moisture, not treating wood. Remediation: Remove affected wood completely (rot spreads from infected wood). Replace with new wood. Treat adjacent wood with rot-preventative (boiled linseed oil, lime, or salt solutions).

Seasonal Woodworking Considerations

Spring (Growth Season)

Optimal tree felling time (sap actively flowing). Bark loosens easily from log. Wood begins drying naturally. Start outdoor projects requiring long drying time. Process green wood for lumber (plan for fall/winter assembly). Begin timber frame preparations. Sawing is easier with green wood (less resistance due to moisture). Plan furniture projects for fall completion.

Summer

Wood drying continues. Begin assembly of components dried over spring/early summer. Maintain projects (apply finishes, inspect structures). Quality of sunlight optimal for fine detailed work (good visibility). Outdoor work comfortable. Complete finishing work while weather stable. Start new timber-frame projects targeting fall construction.

Fall

Finish assembly projects. Apply final finishes before winter (drying conditions stable before cold). Raise timber-frame structures (dry wood + good weather = optimal working conditions). Major construction should begin now (finish before winter weather). Maintain finished projects (inspect for rot, reapply finishes). Begin planning winter projects (indoor furniture, small items).

Winter

Woodworking slows outdoors (cold, short days, wet). Ideal for indoor workshop projects. Sharpen tools (maintenance work). Design and plan spring/summer projects. Build specialized tools and jigs. Prepare wood for spring projects (winter seasoning continues). Small furniture and repair work. Community teaching/training when time available.

Community Carpentry Shop Setup

Minimum Essential Equipment

Hand saws (5-10): Different sizes for different cuts. Budget: 20 hours labor per saw (manufacturing/salvaging). Chisels (8-15): Range of widths (0.5cm to 3cm). Budget: 5 hours labor per chisel. Planes (3-5): Jointer plane (long, straightens surfaces), Jack plane (rough work), Smoothing plane (final finish). Budget: 15 hours per plane (if built from materials; salvage preferable). Mallets (5): Wooden, various weights. Budget: 2 hours each. Clamps (20-30): Rope clamps (cheapest, requires cordage) or wooden screw clamps. Budget: 1-5 hours each. Workbenches (5-8): Sturdy wood tables with vises. Budget: 30-40 hours per bench.

Layout and Organization

Sawing area: Separate from fine work (sawing produces dust, noise). Pit saw setup (two-level bench for two-person sawing). Adequate ventilation. Chisel/plane work area: Benches with vises. Good lighting. Tool storage nearby. Assembly area: Large workspace, clamps accessible, protection from weather. Finishing area: Ventilated, away from sawing dust. Oil/finish storage. Tool sharpening station: Stones, stropping equipment, organized by tool type. Storage: Organized by wood species, lumber dimensions, tools, fasteners. Protect from weather and theft. Moisture-controlled (dry storage optimal).

Staffing and Workflow

Master craftspeople (2-3): Skilled in all techniques, teach apprentices, oversee quality. Journeymen (5-8): Skilled in multiple techniques, lead specific projects, train apprentices. Apprentices (10-15): In training, supervised closely, develop skills through practice. Production flow: (1) Sawyers produce lumber, (2) Joiners cut mortises/tenons, (3) Assemblers put pieces together, (4) Finishers apply oils/paints. Specialization increases production 30-50% after learning curve. Small projects (chairs, boxes): 1-2 months per unit with 2-3 people. Large projects (beds, tables): 3-4 months per unit with 4-5 people. Community of 100+ people can maintain 15-30 craftspeople producing furniture for entire community.

Lumber Processing from Log to Finished Board

Tree felling: Cut at base at 45-degree angle (easier than horizontal cut). Let tree drop naturally; do not try to control fall without experience. Mark best grain direction on log. Allow felled log 2-4 weeks for bark to loosen (easier removal). Bark removal: Use wooden or bone wedge to split bark from log, or soak in water 1-2 weeks allowing easy removal. Begin at butt end where bark naturally separates. Log splitting: Use wedges and maul or axe. Split along grain. Split diameter determines usable pieces: 50cm log yields 4-6 planks; 30cm log yields 2-3 planks. Waste is 15-20%. Sawing planks: Use pit saw (two-person) or hand saw. For critical structural members, saw along grain to create quartersawn planks (more stable, less warping). Standard sawing acceptable for general purposes. Drying: Stack cut lumber with 2cm spacing, protected from weather. Softwood dries in 6-12 weeks; hardwood requires 6-18 months. Air-dry lumber has 12-15% moisture content (acceptable for structures).

Safety Warning: Hand tools sharp enough to cause severe lacerations. Chisels and saws can slice through skin and muscle easily. Never rush or cut toward your body. Secure wood in vise before using both hands on tool. Dull tools are more dangerous (require excessive force, slip unexpectedly). Keep tools properly maintained. Do not work when tired or distracted. Work in groups when possible (witness can assist in emergencies).
Efficiency Tip: Pre-plan all cuts before starting. Mark clearly using knife (not pencil). Create story stick (piece of wood marked with all dimensions) to avoid measuring repeatedly. Batch similar operations (all mortises first, then all tenons). Sharp tools and good technique reduce fatigue and errors dramatically. Invest 30% of time in tool maintenance and planning; this reduces project time 20-30% overall.

Common Mistakes & Solutions

Mistake 1: Insufficient Tool Sharpness

Error: Attempting woodworking with dull tools. Results: forced cuts, tool breakage, wood tearout, poor-fitting joints, extreme fatigue, unexpected slipping and injury.

Solution: Dedicate 30% of woodworking time to tool maintenance. Sharpen chisels, saws, and planes weekly (or more frequently in active use). Dull tools require 3-5x more effort than sharp ones. Test sharpness by cutting arm hair—if doesn't cut easily, sharpen immediately.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Grain Direction

Error: Planing or chiseling against grain direction. Results: deep tearout, rough surface, tool chattering, unsafe tool control, damaged wood requiring resanding.

Solution: Always identify grain direction (usually visible in wood end grain). Examine board end to see grain slope. Plane downslope of grain direction. Work from high grain to low grain. Create test cuts to verify direction before committing to project wood.

Mistake 3: Over-Tightening Test Joints

Error: Driving mortise-tenon joints too tightly during test-fit, jamming them. Results: unable to disassemble, wood splits during removal, joint damaged, test piece ruined.

Solution: Test-fit joints without force. Mark fitting difficulty. Disassemble immediately by gently tapping (backward). Do not fully tap joints until final assembly with glue. Use wooden blocks between hammer and wood to prevent denting.

Mistake 4: Ignoring Wood Movement

Error: Building furniture with wood glued across grain or fully constrained. Wood shrinks seasonally (5-8% across grain), causing splits, warping, or failed joints.

Solution: Allow wood movement in wide boards (floating panels, slotted screw holes). Glue only in grain direction. Accept seasonal dimension changes as normal. Frame-and-panel construction standard solution. Test wood movement in climate by observing existing old furniture movement.

Mistake 5: Poor Layout Accuracy

Error: Sloppy measuring and marking. Results: parts don't fit, joints misaligned, wasted material, frustrating adjustments.

Solution: Spend significant time on layout. Use story stick to avoid repeated measuring. Mark with knife (not pencil) for accuracy. Double-check all dimensions before cutting. Small layout errors compound through project. Create test pieces when learning new techniques.

Mistake 6: Inadequate Clamping During Glue-Up

Error: Insufficient clamps or pressure during gluing. Results: loose joints, glue starvation (glue squeezed out, joint starved), weak assembly.

Solution: Plan clamping strategy before glue-up. Organize clamps beforehand. Apply even pressure across entire joint (not just ends). Use cauls (curved blocks) to distribute pressure evenly. Clamp firmly but don't crush wood. Check that glue appears around joint edges (indicates good glue penetration).

Failure Modes Warning: (1) Poorly executed joinery (gaps, misalignment) = weak joints = structural failure under load. (2) Inadequate understanding of wood movement = seasonal cracking, splitting, failed joints. (3) Using wrong wood type for application (wrong density, durability, rot resistance) = premature failure, collapsed structures. (4) Improper drying (too fast = checking/cracking, too slow = mold, decay) = degraded material. (5) Tool failure due to poor maintenance = dangerous situations, poor quality results, worker injury. (6) Inadequate design (insufficient beam depth, weak connections, missing bracing) = structural collapse. (7) Neglecting wood grain direction in layout = stress concentration, joint failure under load.
Seasonal Consideration: Spring/Summer: fell and process trees during growth season (sap helps remove bark easily, weather supports outdoor work). Fall/Winter: work on finishing/assembly (less wood drying activity required). Plan timber frame projects for late summer (allow 6+ months drying before winter construction). Traditional seasonal carpentry follows natural rhythms: harsh winter weather prevents major construction, so projects scheduled accordingly. Spring tree felling provides summer drying time before fall construction season.
