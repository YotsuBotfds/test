Disaster Preparedness & Emergency Response

Develop community-level disaster preparedness systems and emergency response protocols. Learn to establish early warning systems, execute effective evacuation procedures, construct emergency shelters, and manage response to floods, wildfires, earthquakes, and other catastrophic events. Establish post-disaster recovery organization ensuring community restoration.

Community Early Warning Systems

Early warning systems provide critical minutes or hours for evacuation or sheltering, potentially saving lives. Effective systems rely on observation, communication, and pre-established protocols.

Flood Early Warning System

River and flash flood detection:

Upstream observation posts:

  1. Establish observer positions upstream from settlement (at least 30 minutes travel distance)
  2. Observers watch for rapid water rise, unusual water color (brown, turbid = flood water), or debris in stream
  3. Heavy upstream rainfall may precede visible water changes by 30 minutes to 2 hours
  4. Signal system: predetermined drum/horn signal when flood water detected (e.g., 3 rapid beats = flood warning)

Water level monitoring:

  1. Create graduated marker post on riverbank showing normal water levels at different seasons
  2. Establish baseline: mark normal high water mark from previous years
  3. Unusual rise above historical high mark indicates abnormal conditions
  4. Daily monitoring allows tracking dangerous trends

Community alert response:

Water LevelStatusAction
Below historical highNormalContinue normal activities
At or slightly above historical highWatchIncrease monitoring frequency to every 1-2 hours
20% above historical highAlertStage families near evacuation routes, prepare boats/rafts
40% above historical highWarningBegin evacuation of flood-prone areas to higher ground
Above 40%FloodFull evacuation of low-lying areas, activate emergency shelters

Wildfire Early Warning System

Detection from distance and preparation:

Fire watch stations:

  1. Position elevated observers (hilltops or tall trees) with clear sight lines to surrounding landscape
  2. Observers watch for smoke at any distance (even faint smoke indicates fire)
  3. Observers note fire direction and general distance based on smoke characteristics
  4. Signal alert system immediately upon observation (don't wait to confirm nature of fire)

Wind monitoring:

  1. Strong winds increase fire danger and speed dramatically
  2. Wind direction determines which areas are threatened
  3. Setup simple wind indicator (cloth flag or smoke) to determine direction
  4. Wind speed categories: calm/light (0-3mph, low danger), moderate (4-10mph, medium danger), strong (11-20mph, high danger), severe (20+ mph, extreme danger)

Seasonal fire risk assessment:

Season PhaseRisk LevelPreparedness Action
Rainy season, green vegetationLowRoutine maintenance only
Early dry season, some dead vegetationModerateIncrease watch frequency, review plans
Mid dry season, extensive dry materialHighDaily fire watch, maintain evacuation routes
Late dry season, extreme drynessExtremeContinuous fire watch, pre-position evacuation aids

Earthquake Early Warning (Limited but Possible)

Geological observations (less reliable but informative):

  • Animal behavior changes: unusual animal migration or panic may indicate stress before earthquake
  • Water source changes: springs changing flow rate or turbidity sometimes precedes earthquakes
  • Gas releases: some earthquakes preceded by unusual gas smells from ground
  • Small tremors (foreshocks): series of small earthquakes may precede larger quake

Note: Earthquake prediction is inexact science. Focus on preparedness rather than prediction.

Severe Weather Warning System

Storm detection:

  • Cloud observation: specific cloud formations indicate weather changes (dark towering cumulonimbus = severe thunderstorm)
  • Barometric pressure trends: rapid pressure drop indicates approaching storm
  • Wind changes: sudden wind shift or increase precedes storm arrival
  • Temperature changes: significant temperature drop often precedes severe weather

Alert hierarchy: Develop tiered warning system (watch, warning, emergency) with corresponding community actions for each level.

Evacuation Planning and Execution

Successful evacuation requires planning, training, and practiced execution. Chaotic, unprepared evacuation causes casualties and injury.

Evacuation Plan Development

Essential plan components:

  1. Evacuation routes: Identify 2-3 routes per hazard type. Routes must be passable by all community members (elderly, children, disabled). Routes must lead to safe zones (higher ground for floods, away from fire danger zone for wildfires)
  2. Designated safe zones: Pre-identify meeting locations where community regroups. Zones must be distant enough from hazard (minimum 5 miles for wildfire, minimum 30 feet elevation above 100-year flood level)
  3. Assembly points: Establish first assembly point (close, temporary refuge during initial evacuation) and final assembly point (permanent refuge if long-term evacuation needed)
  4. Responsible persons: Assign individuals responsible for specific actions (sounding alarm, checking designated areas for stragglers, maintaining supplies at safe zone)
  5. Communication system: Method to disseminate evacuation order to all community members (horns, runner system, shouting from position of authority)

Evacuation Scenarios and Drills

Practice evacuation at least twice yearly:

  1. Announce planned evacuation drill 1-2 weeks in advance
  2. Conduct timed evacuation with realistic assumptions (some people absent, some needing assistance)
  3. Time how long complete evacuation requires
  4. Identify bottlenecks or problems
  5. Practice adjusts plan to improve efficiency

Scenario elements to vary:

  • Time of day (night evacuation more challenging than day)
  • Season (winter evacuation with snow/cold more difficult)
  • Weather conditions (rain or high wind impacts routes)
  • Disability simulations (evacuate with simulated injuries/disabilities to verify process works)

Vulnerable Population Evacuation

Special considerations for population subgroups:

PopulationChallengesSolutions
Elderly/infirmSlower movement, may need assistancePre-position strong helpers, possible carrying/dragging methods, staged evacuation starting with most vulnerable first
Children under 5Cannot walk long distances, distracted, may panicAssign multiple adults per child, pre-position carrying devices (slings, baskets), practice calm tone and reassurance
Pregnant womenReduced mobility, may go into laborEnsure evacuation routes accommodate slower pace, position nearest safe zone with birthing knowledge
Animals (pets, livestock)Slow evacuation, difficult to manage in panicPre-train animals to respond to signals, pre-position transport (carts, sleds), accept some animal loss as trade-off

Documentation and Accountability

System for tracking evacuee location:

  1. At first assembly point, designated scribe records names and status of all arrivals
  2. System operates continuously (people arrive over time as they evacuate)
  3. Record any individuals unaccounted for at final assembly point
  4. Information allows search/rescue efforts if needed
  5. Post-evacuation analysis identifies any process failures

Emergency Shelter Construction

When evacuation to pre-built shelter unavailable, community must construct temporary emergency shelter from available materials.

Rapid-Build Group Shelter (50-100 people)

For flood-driven evacuation where return may occur in days:

Materials and construction:

  1. Locate suitable site: elevated location, protected from wind, access to water source
  2. Collect wood: fallen branches, standing dead trees, cut saplings (prioritize use of dead wood to minimize fresh tree cutting)
  3. Create framework: lash poles together to create large A-frame or simple rectangular shelter structure (20-30 feet long, 15-20 feet wide)
  4. Cover framework: use large sections of bark, woven branches (thatching), or cloth/tarps if available. Overlap materials to shed rain
  5. Interior floor: lay branches, straw, or leaves to create raised platform above ground moisture
  6. Divide interior into sections: central cooking area, sleeping areas, storage areas
  7. Build entrance wind-break: small angled structure prevents rain/wind blowing into main shelter

Expected timeline: 20-40 people working 3-4 hours can complete basic shelter for 100+ people.

Individual/Family Emergency Shelters

When group shelter not available, rapid personal shelter:

  1. Lean-to shelter: Long pole lashed horizontally between two trees or supports. Branches leaned against pole, then covered with leaves/bark/cloth. Protects from wind and rain. 2-3 hours construction time for single family shelter
  2. Natural shelter enhancement: If cave, rock overhang, or dense tree growth available, use as foundation. Add minimal material to improve protection
  3. Buried shelter: In ground with minimal materials, dig shallow depression, cover with branches/cloth, leaving opening for entry. Provides wind protection and some thermal benefit

Sheltering Supplies Caches

Pre-positioned emergency shelter materials:

  • Stored at multiple locations along evacuation routes
  • Waterproof containers holding: tarps/cloth, rope, wooden poles bundled, nails/fasteners, tools (axe, saw, knife)
  • Mark locations clearly and train community on cache locations
  • Inspect and rotate supplies annually to maintain readiness

Flood Response and Management

Communities in flood-prone areas require specialized response procedures and preparations.

Flood Defense Measures (Prevention-Focused)

Minimize flood impact through engineering:

MeasureFunctionDifficulty
Levees (earth berms)Create barrier preventing water from flowing into settlementHigh (large-scale construction)
Channels and ditchesRedirect water around settlement to lower-lying areasMedium (community effort)
Elevated settlement areasRelocate critical buildings/infrastructure to higher groundHigh (requires planning)
Flood gates at water sourcesBlock incoming water during flood eventsMedium (requires maintenance)
Permeable ground surfacesReduce runoff by allowing water absorptionLow (avoid paving)

Flood Response Procedures

When flood warning activated:

  1. 2+ hours before flood arrival (Alert stage): Move livestock to high ground, secure loose materials, activate backup water supplies, position boats/rafts for potential rescue
  2. 30+ minutes before flood arrival (Warning stage): Begin evacuation of flood-prone areas to designated high ground, activate emergency shelters, position rescue personnel
  3. During flood (Flood stage): Monitor water levels, maintain rescue readiness, account for all community members, prevent panic
  4. After water recedes (Recovery stage): Assess damage, begin cleanup, identify and address injuries, stabilize buildings/structures

Flood Recovery and Cleanup

Post-flood assessment priorities:

  1. Check water sources for contamination; treat as unsafe until tested
  2. Identify damaged buildings for structural integrity (may be unsafe to enter)
  3. Remove stagnant water and silt from buildings/storage areas
  4. Assess food supplies: discard food exposed to floodwater
  5. Document damage for future reconstruction planning

Wildfire Defense and Response

Wildfire scenarios require different preparation than flood scenarios. Fire moves fast and can be unpredictable.

Defensible Space Development

Reduce fuel around settlement (minimizes fire spread risk):

ZoneDistance from BuildingClearing Work
Zone 0 (Immediate)0-5 feetRemove all dead vegetation, branches. Keep green plants trimmed. Remove leaves/needles from roof
Zone 1 (Intermediate)5-30 feetRemove dead/dying trees and shrubs. Thin dense vegetation. Remove low branches from remaining trees (10+ feet above ground)
Zone 2 (Extended)30-100 feetSelective thinning of trees. Remove ladder fuels (dense vegetation creating fire pathway to tree canopies)
Fuel Removal Priority: In severe wildfire danger, focus on Zone 0 and Zone 1. Extended zone work beneficial but lower priority than immediate protection measures.

Hardening Structures Against Fire

Building modifications to increase fire resistance:

  • Roof materials: Replace wood shingles with fire-resistant materials (clay tile, slate, or non-combustible metal)
  • Exterior walls: Use stone, clay, or non-combustible siding rather than wood
  • Openings: Seal gaps in walls/roof where embers can enter. Use metal mesh in vents
  • Gutters: Keep clean of debris to prevent burning material accumulation
  • Decks: Build from fire-resistant materials if possible

Wildfire Evacuation Response

When wildfire evacuation order issued:

  1. Immediately upon alert: Begin gathering critical belongings (documents, medicines, irreplaceable items), prepare vehicles, activate evacuation routes
  2. Leaving property: Leave doors/windows open (reduces explosive pressure from radiant heat), shut off natural gas at meter, leave outdoor lights on (helps rescuers locate structures in smoke)
  3. Evacuation movement: Move deliberately but quickly. Do not attempt to outrun fire in vehicle (fire can move faster than cars). If evacuation route blocked, shelter in place in building or open area away from burnable vegetation
  4. After evacuation: Do not return to area until all-clear signal issued by authorities. Fire danger persists 12-48 hours after flame front passes due to smoldering ground fuels

Wildfire Mop-Up and Recovery

Post-fire community assessment:

  1. Inspect structures for damage (structural integrity, hidden hot spots)
  2. Clear ash and debris from roofs, gutters, property
  3. Monitor for renewed fire in stumps, roots (can smolder for weeks)
  4. Assess vegetation damage: determine what recovers and what requires replanting
  5. Begin defensible space restoration for future seasons

Earthquake Response

Earthquakes cannot be predicted or prevented. Response focuses on immediate safety and post-earthquake assessment.

During Earthquake

Personal safety actions (every individual trained to execute):

  1. First action: If indoors and away from windows/heavy objects, stay in place and drop to ground. Cover head with hands
  2. If near heavy object/ceiling collapse risk: Move away quickly if possible without running (focus on controlled movement)
  3. If outdoors: Move away from buildings, utility lines, steep slopes. Drop to ground and cover head
  4. If driving: Continue driving at safe speed to clear area, avoid stopping near buildings/power lines
  5. High-rise building: Stay in place unless fire or obvious structural damage occurs. Do not use elevators (power may fail mid-floor)

Immediate Post-Earthquake Actions (First 30 Minutes)

Community emergency response:

  1. Community leaders assess immediate damage: injuries, structural damage to critical buildings, utility disruptions
  2. Activate emergency signal system to alert community to report for duty
  3. Organize rescue operations: identify trapped individuals, organize careful extrication
  4. Assess water/sanitation systems: check for contamination, activate backup supplies
  5. Identify medical emergency: triage injured, position at medical treatment area

Extended Response (Hours to Days)

Sustained disaster management:

PriorityActionTimeline
SafetyRescue trapped people, stabilize structures, clear hazardsFirst 24 hours
HealthProvide first aid, manage disease prevention, address sanitationFirst week
ShelterAssess building damage, relocate displaced persons, provide emergency shelterFirst 3 days
Water/FoodActivate emergency supplies, restore water systems, distribute foodFirst week
CommunicationEstablish communication systems, share information, reduce panicContinuous

Building Safety Inspection

Trained inspectors assess earthquake damage:

  1. Visual inspection for structural damage (cracks, misalignment, deterioration)
  2. Check for utility damage (natural gas leaks particularly dangerous)
  3. Assess collapse risk (visible damage overhead)
  4. Mark buildings: green (safe), yellow (caution, assess further), red (danger, do not enter)

Post-Disaster Recovery Organization

Recovery from major disaster requires sustained organizational effort extending weeks to months or longer.

Recovery Organization Structure

Command and control system:

  1. Recovery coordinator: Designated leader directing all recovery operations, coordinating between different teams
  2. Recovery assessment team: Documents all damage, creates rebuilding priorities, tracks resources
  3. Rescue and medical team: Provides medical care to injured, locates missing persons
  4. Shelter and logistics team: Manages emergency shelter, food distribution, sanitation
  5. Infrastructure repair team: Restores water systems, addresses contamination, repairs utilities

Damage Assessment and Prioritization

Critical decisions during recovery:

Damage CategoryAssessment PriorityRecovery Priority
Water systemsFirst (safe water critical)First (health dependent)
Medical/health infrastructureFirst (injury treatment)Second (health maintenance)
Food systemsSecondSecond (starvation risk)
Housing/shelterSecondThird (emergency shelter temporary)
Communication systemsThird (lower risk)Third (recovery efficiency depends)
Cultural/community buildingsLower (non-critical)Fourth (morale, after critical needs)

Recovery Timelines and Planning

Realistic recovery phases:

  1. Phase 1 (Days 1-7): Emergency Response - Focus on immediate safety, medical care, emergency shelter, water safety
  2. Phase 2 (Weeks 2-4): Stabilization - Food systems restoration, disease prevention, initial damage assessment, housing stabilization
  3. Phase 3 (Months 2-6): Rebuilding - Reconstruction planning, infrastructure repair, permanent shelter solutions, economic activity restart
  4. Phase 4 (Months 6+): Recovery Completion - Long-term rebuilding, psychological recovery, preparation for future disasters

Resource Management

Managing limited post-disaster resources:

  • Inventory all available resources: food, water, medical supplies, tools, building materials, personnel skills
  • Prioritize allocation toward health and safety needs first
  • Establish fair distribution system preventing hoarding and conflict
  • Account for vulnerable populations needing additional support
  • Consider community members' specialized skills for key recovery roles (medical, carpentry, leadership)

Community Morale During Recovery

Psychological support during extended recovery:

  • Regular communication from leadership reducing rumors and panic
  • Clear recovery plans showing progress and hope for future
  • Celebrate small accomplishments and progress milestones
  • Maintain cultural/spiritual practices providing psychological continuity
  • Musical activities help maintain mental health during extended recovery
  • Recognize contributions of recovery workers to maintain morale
