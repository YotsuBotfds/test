Traditional Joinery Without Nails

Nail-free joinery relies on precisely fitted joints locked by wood-to-wood contact and natural material binding. These techniques have sustained buildings and furniture for millennia and require only basic hand tools.

Mortise and Tenon Joint (Foundation Technique)

The mortise and tenon is the strongest wood-to-wood connection available and forms the basis for quality furniture construction.

Components:

  • Tenon: projection cut on end of one board
  • Mortise: matching rectangular hole cut in receiving board
  • Together they create mechanical interlock stronger than fasteners alone

Cutting mortise:

  1. Mark mortise location with careful measurements from edges
  2. Using chisel (1/2 inch to 1 inch width), make series of cuts to depth
  3. Work from center outward, striking chisel firmly with mallet
  4. After series of cuts, use chisel held flat to clear debris from bottom
  5. Final passes with sharp chisel create flat bottom and straight sides
  6. Test tenon fit—should slide in with slight resistance, not loose

Cutting tenon:

  1. Mark tenon shoulders (outline of remaining wood around projection)
  2. Using saw, cut along grain on all four sides to depth
  3. Use chisel to clean up sides and create flush, flat tenon face
  4. Tenon should fit snugly in mortise—this tight fit is strength, not problem

Lap Joint (Frame and Grid Construction)

Simpler than mortise-tenon, lap joints work where boards cross at surface level.

Process:

  1. Mark overlap area on both boards (typically half the board thickness)
  2. Using saw, make cuts along each side of marked area to proper depth
  3. Use chisel to remove waste wood between cuts
  4. Boards should fit together with flush surfaces
  5. Binding with cord or natural glues locks joint

Dovetail Joint (Decorative and Structural)

Dovetails are most visible in traditional furniture construction and add both strength and aesthetic appeal.

Dovetail layout: Create pattern of angled cuts (typically 8-10 degrees) that interlock. Each board has interlocking pattern mirrored from other.

Process:

  1. Mark tails (projections) on first board
  2. Saw carefully along angled lines to proper depth
  3. Use board with tails as template to mark pins (recesses) on receiving board
  4. Saw pins, being very careful of accuracy
  5. Test fit and adjust with chisel as needed
  6. Final assembly: coat with natural glue, drive together with mallet and protective block

Natural Wood Binding (Without Glue)

High-quality wood joints rely on fit, not fasteners. However, supplementary binding increases longevity:

  • Plant fiber cord (hemp, flax, nettle): wrap tightly around joint, seal with wax
  • Rawhide strips: wet rawhide shrinks as it dries, creating mechanical lock
  • Birch bark strips: flexible, water-resistant natural binding
  • Wooden wedges: drive into mortise-tenon joint to lock permanently

Adhesives for Wood Joinery

Animal glue (hot hide glue): Traditional woodworking adhesive. Boil animal hides/bones in water 4-8 hours, collecting resulting gelatinous liquid. Heat before use. Sets in 24 hours. Reversible with moisture/heat (useful for future repairs).

Plant-based adhesive: Boil starchy plants (tree bark, roots, tubers) in water until extremely thick, glutinous paste forms. Less reliable than animal glue but biodegradable and sufficient for moderate-duty joints.

Natural latex: Sap from certain trees (rubber, fig) creates tacky adhesive. Not as strong as animal glue but useful for non-structural bonding.

Bed and Sleeping Platform Construction

Proper sleep support prevents back injury and improves rest quality. Build sleeping platforms from wood to last decades.

Simple Post-and-Frame Platform

Materials (for 6-foot by 4-foot platform):

ComponentDimensionsQuantityNotes
Posts (corner)4x4 inches, 36 inches tall4Supports weight, allows storage underneath
Longitudinal beams2x8 inches, 72 inches long2Runs lengthwise
Lateral beams2x8 inches, 48 inches long2Connects sides
Support slats2x4 inches, 48 inches long6-8Spaced 6-8 inches apart

Construction steps:

  1. Cut four corner posts to identical length (36 inches)
  2. Create mortises at top of each post to accept beams (mortise 1 inch deep, 7.5 inches wide, 3.5 inches tall)
  3. Create matching tenons on beam ends
  4. Assemble frame: insert longitudinal beams into side posts first, then add lateral beams, checking for square
  5. Wedge all mortise-tenon joints and bind with cord
  6. Attach support slats across interior of frame using mortises or lap joints, spacing evenly
  7. Test structure under weight before adding sleeping surface

Sleeping Surface Options

Wooden slat platform: Overlapping thick wooden slats provide support and allow air circulation. Finish with straw mattress or cloth padding.

Rope bed: Weave rope across frame in grid pattern (rope passed through holes bored in beam sides). Rope can be tightened if sagging occurs.

Woven platform: Create lattice of branches or saplings bound with cord, creating elastic sleeping surface.

Mattress Construction

Straw tick: Sew linen or heavy cloth into large rectangular pocket. Fill with dried straw or grass. Sew shut. Requires regular emptying and restuffing (yearly).

Wool or cotton batting: Carded and layered wool creates comfortable, resilient mattress. Layer 4-6 inches on frame, compress gradually. Wool prevents dust mite proliferation better than straw.

Rope tensioning: If using rope bed, rope naturally sags. Provide mechanism to tighten: wooden turnbuckle at corners or adjustable rope routing allows retensioning periodically.

Headboard and Footboard

Practical and supportive additions:

  1. Create vertical board (1-1.5 inches thick, 36-48 inches tall)
  2. Mortise into top corners of bed posts
  3. Finish with decorative carving if desired
  4. Provides back support for sitting in bed and prevents pillows falling

Tables and Benches

Trestle Table (Fastest, Most Versatile)

Materials (6-foot table):

ComponentDimensionsQuantity
Top board1.5 inches thick, 12 inches wide, 72 inches long1
Trestle legs2x6 inches, 30 inches tall4
Trestle top rails2x6 inches, 18 inches long2
Stretchers2x4 inches, 60 inches long1-2

Construction:

  1. Create two trestle supports (A-frame shape each)
  2. Each trestle: two legs joined at angle with mortise-tenon, cross-braced with stretcher
  3. Trestle tops mortised to receive stretcher
  4. Top board rests on trestle supports without fasteners (weight holds in place)
  5. Table can be disassembled by lifting top and removing trestles

Fixed Leg Table

More permanent design:

  1. Create four legs (2x3 inches, 28-30 inches tall)
  2. Create perimeter frame from 2x4 inch boards using mortise-tenon joints
  3. Mortise legs into frame corners
  4. Attach top with wooden buttons or wedges allowing seasonal wood movement
  5. Add internal stretchers if table wider than 3 feet (prevents racking)

Bench Seating (Backless or Backed)

Simple bench (12 feet long):

  1. Create two leg assemblies (each with 2x4 legs joined with mortises)
  2. Connect leg assemblies with stretchers
  3. Attach seat boards across top
  4. Optional: Create backrest frame mortised into rear of seat structure

Settee (bench with high back and arms):

  1. Build basic bench structure
  2. Add posts at arm height and ends (4x4 inches minimum)
  3. Mortise arm supports into posts
  4. Create tall backrest frame (vertical slats joined with horizontal rails)
  5. More complex construction requires careful measuring and accurate joinery

Storage Solutions

Shelving Systems

Wall-mounted shelves (most practical):

  1. Install horizontal wall supports (1x6 or 2x6 inch boards) anchored into studs with wooden pegs or mortises
  2. Create shelf boards of desired length and thickness
  3. Shelves rest on cleats (supports running perpendicular to wall)
  4. Shelves can be fixed (mortised to cleats) or floating (simply resting)
  5. Floating shelves are easier to adjust; fixed shelves hold more weight

Freestanding shelving units:

  1. Create frame structure with vertical supports and horizontal stretchers
  2. Mount shelves using mortises, lap joints, or simple cleats
  3. Ensure unit is braced well to prevent tipping (critical if full of heavy items)
  4. Diagonal bracing or back panel improves rigidity

Chest construction steps:

  1. Build box frame using mortise-tenon joints at all corners
  2. Create bottom frame inside box with feet projecting down
  3. Attach side planks with mortises into corner posts
  4. Create lid frame with dovetails for strength
  5. Mortise leather hinges (or wooden pivot) into lid and box edges
  6. Add internal storage dividers if desired
  7. Finish wood with oil to protect from moisture

Drawer Construction

Essential components:

  1. Drawer box: four-sided construction with dovetail corners
  2. Bottom panel: thin board fitted into grooves (plow) in box sides
  3. Guides: wooden tracks that drawer sides ride on (minimize friction)
  4. Stops: small cleats prevent drawer from falling out when pulled fully open
  5. Pull: carved or shaped wooden handle mortised into front face

Drawer success depends on accurate, square construction. Test fit frequently during building.

Household Utensils from Wood

Bowls and Dishes

Turned bowls (on lathe or carved by hand):

  1. Select wood grain appropriate for bowls (birch, maple, ash)
  2. Rough-shape exterior with axe or drawknife
  3. If lathe available: mount and turn to desired shape
  4. If hand-carving: use gouge chisels to hollow interior, starting from center
  5. Sand smooth with fine grit when complete
  6. Finish with food-safe oil (beeswax, linseed oil)

Spoons and Utensils

Carved spoon method:

  1. Select straight-grained wood piece (3/4 inch thick minimum)
  2. Draw spoon shape on wood blank
  3. Rough cut shape with axe/saw
  4. Use gouge chisel to carve bowl of spoon
  5. Shape handle with drawknife and sandstone
  6. Finish with oil and beeswax mixture

Utensil designs: Large serving spoons for cooking, smaller eating spoons, forks (carved with tines), ladles (large bowl with long handle), and stirring spoons all follow same basic carving principles.

Cutting and Chopping Boards

Simple board:

  1. Select dense hardwood (oak, maple) cut roughly 1.5 inches thick
  2. Create rectangular shape with saw and plane
  3. Round edges slightly for comfort and easier cleaning
  4. Finish with food-safe oil
  5. Create groove around perimeter with gouge to catch juices (optional)

Maintenance: Oil regularly (weekly with food oil) to prevent cracking and mold. Scrub with salt and lemon juice after use.

Broom and Cleaning Tool Making

Broom Construction (Tied Bundle Method)

Materials:

  • Natural bristle material (broom corn, marsh grass, birch twigs, heather)
  • Wooden handle (1.5 inches diameter, 48-60 inches long)
  • Binding material (rope, twine, or cloth strips)
  • Metal ferrule (optional, prevents fraying)

Process:

  1. Gather bristle material and dry completely (hanging in bundles)
  2. Create handle: carve or turn to ergonomic thickness (thinner at grip end)
  3. Prepare bristles: soak briefly in water to make pliable
  4. Bundle bristles around handle end, starting with small core bundle
  5. Add bristles in expanding layers, maintaining roughly cylindrical shape
  6. Bind tightly with rope at 3-4 points along bristle length
  7. Allow to dry in upright position
  8. Trim bristle ends to uniform length (scissors or sharp knife)
  9. Create second binding point 6-8 inches from tip to define broom head shape

Bristle Material Selection

Broom corn (traditional): Stiff, effective sweeping, lasts 6-12 months with regular use. Best for outdoor/rough sweeping.

Marsh grass (softer alternative): More flexible, suitable for fine sweeping. Shorter lifespan (4-6 months).

Birch twigs (emergency): Serviceable but shorter-lived (2-4 months). Works for rough cleaning only.

Heather and dried herbs: Decorative and slightly scented. Moderate durability.

Specialized Cleaning Tools

Toilet Brush

Construction:

  1. Use softer bristle material (horse hair if available, or dried plant fibers)
  2. Create smaller, rounder brush head (2-3 inches diameter)
  3. Attach to long handle (12-15 inches) using same binding method as brooms
  4. Make small wooden collar that allows brush to stand upright in holder

Dusting Brush

For fine dust removal:

  1. Use very fine hair (human hair collected from combing, or plant fibers)
  2. Create flatter, wider brush head (4-6 inches wide)
  3. Attach to shorter, lighter handle (12-18 inches)
  4. Looser binding allows bristles to move freely

Scrubbing Brush

For heavy-duty cleaning:

  1. Use coarse, stiff bristles (boar hair, palm fiber, or stiff plant fibers)
  2. Create compact brush head (3x4 inches)
  3. Bind very tightly to maximize durability
  4. Attach wooden handle for leverage

Holder and Storage

Broom storage:

  1. Create simple wall-mounted rack with pegs or wooden hooks
  2. Store bristles up to prevent deformation
  3. Allow complete air drying between use
  4. Inspect regularly for mold or deterioration
