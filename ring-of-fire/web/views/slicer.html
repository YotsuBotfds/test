Reading a slab

A sinking plate stays colder than the mantle around it for millions of years, so it can still break in earthquakes hundreds of kilometres down. Those quakes trace the slab. Dip is its angle below the horizontal.

Each dot is a quake within the band, sized by magnitude and coloured by depth. Dots fade the farther they lie from the line, and quakes shallower than 70 km are drawn a little dimmer. Where there is room, bars beside the section count quakes in each slice of depth.

Steep slabs like the Mariana sink under their own weight and pull the sea floor behind them apart. Shallow slabs like Japan's grind against the plate above, and that tight contact is where the largest megathrust earthquakes happen.

No quake in this catalogue is deeper than —. Below about 700 km the slab still sinks, but it no longer breaks. Switch to Down to the core to see how much mantle lies below.

Quakes within the band are projected onto the plane of the great circle, keeping their true depth. The fit uses quakes 70 km deep or more. RANSAC over pairs of quakes finds the best-supported dipping line, then an orthogonal regression with Tukey biweights refines it. A quadratic replaces the line where the slab bends and that cuts the scatter by 12% or more. Dip is measured from the shallowest to the deepest fitting quake, against the local horizontal; the ± is the formal 1σ error. Scatter is the RMS distance of fitting quakes from the line. Teal marks are plate boundaries from Bird (2003). Wadati traced the sloping zone under Japan in 1935, Benioff around the Pacific in 1954; the steep and shallow slab types follow Uyeda and Kanamori (1979).
