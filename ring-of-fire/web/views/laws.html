Three Laws

One earthquake is unpredictable. Twenty-three thousand of them follow rules. Small quakes outnumber big ones by a fixed ratio, aftershocks fade as a power of time, and nearly all the energy comes out in a few giants. Each law below is fitted live to this catalogue, and every number is computed from it.

Method. Magnitudes are reported to 0.1. b is the maximum-likelihood estimate of Aki (1965) with Utsu's half-bin correction, b = log₁₀e / (M̄ − (Mc − 0.05)), and its error is from Shi & Bolt (1982). Completeness is estimated three ways: maximum curvature plus 0.2 (Wiemer & Wyss 2000; Woessner & Wiemer 2005), goodness of fit at 95% or else 90% of cumulative counts explained (Wiemer & Wyss 2000), and b-value stability over 0.5 magnitude units (Cao & Gao 2002); the largest is used. Magnitude types were counted from the USGS magType field. Aftershocks are quakes of M ≥ Mc within one subsurface rupture length of the epicentre (log L = −2.44 + 0.59 M, Wells & Coppersmith 1994; at least 50 km), for 180 days or until a quake as large strikes the same zone. The rate λ(t) = μ + K/(c+t)p + Σ Kj/(c+t−tj)p, with a separate term for each aftershock of M ≥ max(M₀ − 1.5, 7) after the first day, is fitted by maximum likelihood (Ogata 1983) with the background μ fixed at the median 30-day count in the zone away from the sequence. Errors come from the observed information matrix; the residual check is a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of the transformed times (Ogata 1988). Rates use four log bins per decade with Gehrels (1986) 1σ limits. Energy follows log E = 1.5 M + 4.8 in joules (Gutenberg & Richter 1956), applied to the catalogue's own magnitudes. The map is a squarified treemap (Bruls, Huizing & van Wijk 2000) with cushion shading (van Wijk & van de Wetering 1999); the hairline gaps take a sliver of each tile, nothing else.
